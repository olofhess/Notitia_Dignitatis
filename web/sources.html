Sources

The Latin text remains the source. Modern geography and translation are interpretative layers.

Primary Latin text

Living Notitia follows the scholarly text tradition established by Otto Seeck in his 1876 edition, Notitia dignitatum: accedunt Notitia urbis Constantinopolitanae et Laterculi provinciarum. The digital transcription used as the project's textual basis was prepared by Halstein Sjølie and is available through The Latin Library.

Ancient geography

Ancient-place identification and coordinates use Pleiades as a principal gazetteer. The interactive map is displayed with Leaflet and OpenStreetMap base-map data. Geographic identifications remain an interpretative layer rather than part of the Latin source.

English rendering

The English layer is a reading aid developed for Living Notitia. It is intended to make offices, units and relationships easier to explore; it is not presented as a critical translation and should not replace consultation of the Latin text.

Roman roads — a work in progress

Living Notitia includes a preliminary reconstruction of road routes transcribed from itinerary tables in the Bodleian manuscript material used in this project. The tables record sequences of places and distances, generally in Roman miles, rather than surveyed road alignments.

The routes were transcribed, matched to ancient places using Pleiades and other geographical references, assembled into ordered geographical sequences, and processed in QGIS before export to GeoJSON. This iterative, AI-assisted workflow allows individual identifications and route sequences to be revised as the research develops.

The present layer contains 374 line features, not 374 separately attested historical roads. It is incomplete: gaps, uncertain identifications and schematic connections remain. Lines indicate reconstructed connections between itinerary stations, not the precise physical courses of Roman roads.

The roads can be compared with provincial boundaries and places recorded in the Notitia Dignitatum. For example, the stations under Dux Thebaidos can be studied alongside routes in the Nile valley and towards the Red Sea. The sources are distinct; proximity alone does not establish a historical connection.

Workflow: transcription → place identification → coordinate matching → route reconstruction → QGIS → GeoJSON → interactive map.

Editorial principle: Ancient names and the Latin text are primary. Modern names, coordinates, classifications and translations are aids to navigation and analysis.
